The Math Behind Every Number

A plain-and-precise walkthrough of how the Jordan real-estate recommendation engine turns raw market listings into the numbers you see on screen. One section per view of the app — documented as we visit each. Every constant below is copied verbatim from app/engine/config.py; nothing here is illustrative.

Evidence-backed Sales-comparison approach Cost & income cross-checks Confidence scoring Scenario simulation No hand-set prices
Core principle. The engine never sets a price by hand. Every number is derived from real listings and documented assumptions, and every derived number carries its own method, sample size, source, and confidence. This document opens the box on that derivation, view by view.

1 Pricing

The Pricing view answers one question: what should this specific unit launch at? It shows, in order: the three prices, a confidence breakdown, the comparables the price rests on, two scenario tools (What-if & Compare), and a plain-language summary. Below is the math behind each.

Min-safe
the floor you shouldn't go below
Recommended
the evidence-backed launch price
Premium
the top the evidence can defend

1.1  The three prices

Everything anchors on one number derived from the comparable listings: the reconciled asking price per m² for this submarket (how that is built → §1.3). From there:

Recommended price

Asking prices sit above what units actually transact for. The client methodology PDF documents a 5–15% ask-to-transaction gap; the engine applies the midpoint (10%) uniformly and surfaces it as its own sourced evidence row — it is a documented assumption, not a derived premium.

# mid_gap = (0.05 + 0.15) / 2 = 0.10
recommended = comparison_asking × (1 − mid_gap)  = asking × 0.90

Min-safe price

The lowest defensible launch price. It starts at the bottom of the confidence band (§1.2), is lifted to the cost-approach floor if that is higher (a rational buyer won't pay below the cost to rebuild: replacement construction cost minus depreciation, ≈ 0 for new build), and is never allowed to exceed the recommended price.

min_safe = min(  max( confidence.low,  cost_floor ) ,  recommended  )

Premium price

The most the evidence can defend — the top of the confidence band. If the income approach (what investors will pay: annual rent ÷ required yield) sets a higher ceiling, the premium is allowed to reach it; it never falls below the recommended price. Crucially, the premium is not a fabricated number above the evidenced range.

premium = max(  confidence.high  |  income_ceiling (if higher) ,  recommended  )
# income_ceiling = annual_local_rent ÷ required_yield
# required_yield = citywide annual rent ÷ citywide sale price/m²  (fallback 6.5%)
Why three, not one? A single number hides risk. The spread between min-safe and premium is the honest uncertainty of the submarket — wide where data is thin, tight where comps agree. The width comes directly from the confidence math below.

1.2  Confidence

Confidence scores the reliability of the recommendation (distinct from the trust rating on any single source). The headline 0–100 score is a weighted blend of five reliability signals; the app then shows the four most decision-relevant of them as bars.

The headline score

score = 0.28·density + 0.24·dispersion + 0.18·recency + 0.18·method_agreement + 0.12·source_quality
SignalWeightHow it is computed (from real comps)
Data density0.281.0 at 8+ comps; 0.1 at 1 comp; linear between. More comps → higher.
Dispersion0.24max(0, 1 − cv / 0.35). Tighter spread of adjusted comps → higher. At ≥35% variation → 0.
Recency0.18max(0, 1 − avg_age_months / 18). Fresher listings → higher.
Method agreement0.18Do comparison, cost & income converge? max(0, 1 − spread / 0.6). <2 methods → neutral 0.5.
Source quality0.12Average source trust of the comps (H=1.0, M=0.6, L=0.3).
# label from the 0–1 score
score ≥ 0.70 → High    score ≥ 0.45 → Medium    else → Low

The four bars you see

The app decomposes the score into four plain-language bars (each 0–100 with a one-line reason). A bar that can't be computed from the evidence is shown as "not scorable" — never faked.

BarMaps toReads as
Data coveragedensity × 100"N comparables after outlier filtering; full weight at 8+"
Comparable qualitydispersion × 100"adjusted comparables vary X% (tighter = higher quality)"
Official evidencegov signalsCBJ price index present/current + construction-cost floor available
Market freshnessrecency × 100"average comparable age ~N months (ideal ≤12)"
What changed. A fifth bar, Future signals, was removed — the product is now date-backed evidence only. It never contributed to the headline score (that was always the five reliability signals above), so removing the bar does not change any price or score. It was purely explanatory, and now it's gone.

How the band width is set

The gap between min-safe and premium is the confidence interval around the recommended price. Its half-width is the observed weighted dispersion of the comps, widened as confidence falls, then clamped so a freak-tight or freak-wide sample can't produce an absurd range.

base = weighted_std(adjusted comps) ÷ point  # or cv if no comps
if comps < 3: base = max(base, 0.15)  # thin market: no false precision
widen = 1 + (1 − score)  # low confidence → up to 2× width
half = clamp( base × widen,  0.04,  0.35 )
band = point × (1 ± half)

1.3  Comparables & reconciliation

"The listings this price is based on." Every recommendation is built from real nearby apartments. Turning a pile of listings into one reconciled asking price/m² is a four-step pipeline:

1. Select — nearby, similar-size, recent listings for the same property type
↓
2. De-duplicate — collapse cross-syndicated relistings of the same physical unit
↓
3. Adjust — normalise each comp to your unit (view, floor, beds, age, finish, size)
↓
4. Reconcile — weighted mean, blended toward the submarket median

Selection knobs (from the client PDF, not prices)

KnobValueMeaning
Recency window18 monthsOldest a listing can be and still count (ideal ≤12).
Size band±45%Prefer comps within this of your area; relaxes in 25% steps if too few.
Thin-market floor3 compsBelow this the market is "thin" and reliability drops.
Full-confidence8 compsAt/above this, low count stops penalising confidence.
Target12 compsStop selecting once this many strong comps are found.

Adjustments are derived, then capped

There is no hard-coded "view = +8%" anywhere. Each premium (view, floor, beds, age, finish, size) is derived from the market at runtime by paired-listing comparison, then bounded — large adjustments reduce reliability, so v1 keeps them modest and honest.

Adjustment typeCap
One categorical attribute (view, parking, finish…)±20%
Continuous (floor, age)±12%
Per-m² size curve±10%
Net (sum of all adjustments on one comp)±40%  flagged low-reliability beyond 30%

Reconciliation weight

Comps are combined by a weighted mean. A comp is worth more if it is fresh and needed little adjustment. The 0.20 floor caps how much any single near-identical comp can dominate (max/min weight ratio ≈ 3×).

weight = recency ÷ ( 0.20 + gross_adjustment )
Submarket-median anchor. Once the pool is large (≥24 rows — twice the target), the small top-similarity draw is blended toward the pool's robust median by inverse-variance weighting, so a lucky/unlucky handful of comps can't pull the estimate off the submarket's true centre. Below that, the comps already are the submarket.

1.4  What-if & Compare (scenario tools)

Both tools answer "how does the price move if I change something?" — and they do it the honest way: by re-running the exact same engine on a modified input, never by nudging a price directly. An override that changes nothing yields a zero delta.

What-if — one scenario vs base

Levers map to real engine inputs:

LeverHow it flows through the engine
finish / parking / view / beds / age / target floorChange the Subject → re-derive adjustments → new band.
construction cost ±%Scales the cost benchmarks → moves the cost-approach floor (min-safe & method-agreement).
launch delay (months)Advances as_of → every comp ages → recency falls.
recommended_delta = ( scenario.recommended − base.recommended ) ÷ base.recommended × 100%

The same delta is reported for min-safe, premium, and the confidence score, with a plain-language line listing exactly which levers were applied.

Compare — 2 to 4 scenarios side by side

Each named scenario is run through the identical simulation, so the deltas are directly comparable. The tool flags best recommended and safest floor across the set.

The guarantee. "Every lever flows through the engine's own evidence — no price was set by hand." If you can't move a number through a documented mechanism, the scenario tools won't move it at all.

2 Opportunity

The Opportunity view answers: where should I build? Every covered area gets a single 0–100 opportunity score — a weighted blend of four components, each scored relative to all other areas. It is a comparison, not an absolute: 50 means "middle of the pack", not "50% good".

Price headroom
how pricey (margin room)
Price growth
is it appreciating
Sale demand
density & activity
Low competition
how uncrowded

2.1  The shared step: percentile rank

Each component computes a raw value per area, then converts it to 0–100 by percentile rank — where the area sits in the ranking of all areas:

score(area) = ( count of areas with a LOWER raw value ) ÷ ( total areas − 1 ) × 100
Why rank, not min-max? The data has garbage outliers — one area's median "sale" is 120,000 JD/m² (a bad row). Min-max scaling would peg that to 100 and crush every real area toward 0 (Abdoun's true 91st-percentile price collapsed to 1). Percentile rank asks only "how does this area stand vs the others", so a single absurd tail can't distort the scale. The lowest area → 0, the highest → 100, everyone else spreads evenly.

2.2  The four components

1. Price headroom weight 0.30 — raw uses median

raw = median( plausible sale price/m² of the area )  # outlier-filtered: drop <250 JD/m², trim tails

Higher median price → more gross-development-value headroom. Percentile-ranked across areas.

2. Price growth weight 0.25 — raw uses median, tiered & mix-adjusted

Growth needs dated sales, and this corpus is clustered (lots of dated sales 2017–2023, a ~2-year gap, then a handful in 2026). So the window is recent year vs 1–3 years ago (a wide older window that actually reaches the historical data — an adjacent 12-month window lands in the empty gap). Two tiers:

# Tier 1 — like-for-like (mix-adjusted): compare only SIMILAR units
group sales by (size band 50m², bedrooms); in each bucket with ≥3 sales/side:
  bucket_growth = ( median(recent) − median(older) ) ÷ median(older) × 100
growth = sample-weighted mean of bucket growths

# Tier 2 — fallback when too sparse to match buckets (≥5 dated sales/side)
growth = ( median(recent) − median(older) ) ÷ median(older) × 100  # unadjusted, labelled as such

Median, not mean — property prices are right-skewed, so a few luxury outliers would drag a mean. Like-for-like removes the illusion where a change in what got listed masquerades as price movement. Areas without enough dated sales on both sides show n/a (and drop from the score).

Data reality: only ~15 areas currently have enough dated sales on both sides. The full unlock is the DLS dated-transaction data (official sales), which turns this into a dense, trustworthy index.

3. Sale demand weight 0.30 — raw uses mean of two ranked proxies

raw = mean(  rank(median population_density) ,  rank(median night_light_radiance)  )

Population density and satellite night-light radiance (urban activity) are carried per listing and aggregated by median per area, then each is percentile-ranked and the two are averaged (whichever are present). Denser / brighter area → stronger demand signal. Areas whose listings carry no spatial data show n/a.

4. Low competition weight 0.15 — raw is a count, ranked inverted

raw = active listing count in the area
score = 100 − rank(raw)  # inverted: fewer listings → higher score

More competing supply → lower score. (No competing-project data exists yet, so the proxy is listings only; projects re-enter when that data lands.) Note: this is absolute supply, so the single most-listed area in the country scores 0 — correctly, it faces the most competition.

2.3  The composite score

opportunity = Σ( weight × component score ) ÷ Σ( weights of components that EXIST )

Weights: price headroom 0.30, price growth 0.25, sale demand 0.30, low competition 0.15. If a component is n/a it drops out and its weight is redistributed — a missing metric never counts as a zero and never drags the score down.

2.4  Worked example — Abdoun (real numbers)

ComponentRawScore (percentile)Weight
Price headroommedian ~1,114 JD/m²91 (premium)0.30
Price growth−18.6% (unadjusted)500.25

The reading is honest and specific: Abdoun is expensive (great margins) with strong demand, but saturated (most listings in Jordan) and not appreciating recently → a solid-but-not-top opportunity. Honesty rules. Every component that can't be computed shows n/a, never a fabricated 0. Bars are relative (0 = lowest in the set, 100 = highest), and each carries a one-line reason with its raw value and source.

3 Economics

The Economics view answers the land question: what is the most I can pay for the land and still hit my target profit? It uses the residual method — start from what the finished building sells for, subtract every cost the developer incurs, and whatever is left over is the ceiling for the land. It is a ceiling, not a market land quote.

GDV — what the finished building sells for
−
Construction · Fees · Finance · Developer profit
↓
Residual land value — the most you can pay for the land

3.1  The formula

residual land value = GDV − Construction − Fees − Finance − Developer profit

The order of the sub-calculations matters — fees feed finance:

GDV = recommended price/m² × buildable area
Construction = build cost/m² × buildable area
Fees = fees% × Construction  # NOT of GDV
Finance = finance% × ( Construction + Fees )  # all deployed capital
Developer profit = profit% × GDV

3.2  Where each number comes from

LineBasisSource of the number
GDV (gross dev value)price/m² × areaprice/m² is the engine's recommended launch price (traceable to §1); area is your input (default 2,000 m²)
Constructioncost/m² × areabuild cost is your input (default 450 JD/m²)
Fees5% of constructiondesign / engineering / permits — documented assumption, overridable
Finance6% of (construction + fees)cost of borrowing during the build — documented assumption, overridable
Developer profit20% of GDVyour required margin — documented assumption, overridable
What's derived vs assumed. Only GDV flows in from the model (the recommended launch price). The three percentages (6% / 5% / 20%) are documented builder assumptions from the client methodology PDF — every one is an editable input, defaulted, not derived. The engine never invents a land price; it computes the break-even ceiling for the assumptions you give it.

3.3  Worked example

Buildable 2,000 m², GDV 1,000 JD/m², build cost 450 JD/m², fees 5%, finance 6%, profit 20%:

GDV = 1,000 × 2,000         =  2,000,000
− Construction = 450 × 2,000          =   −900,000
− Fees = 5% × 900,000      =    −45,000
− Finance = 6% × (900,000+45,000) =   −56,700
− Profit = 20% × 2,000,000   =  −400,000
= Residual land value         =   598,300  (≈ 299 JD per buildable m²)
How to read it. Pay more than ~598,300 JD for this land and you eat into your 20% margin — so it's the builder's maximum defensible land price, not a quote for what land actually trades at. Land above the line turns the target return negative.
Deterministic & consistent. This is a pure formula — same inputs always give the same answer. The live engine (builder.residual_land_value) and the offline fallback now compute it identically (fees on construction, finance on construction + fees), so the land ceiling doesn't change depending on whether the backend is reachable.

4 Monitoring

The Monitoring view is a market-at-a-glance dashboard — a few simple graphs, each with a plain caption, plus an Expand button that fills the whole screen for cross-region comparison. The one rule that makes it trustworthy:

Every value is computed on the server and only rendered in the browser. There is no chart math in the frontend — it receives ready-to-plot numbers from GET /area-monitoring (built in app/engine/monitoring.py) and draws bars. If the engine is unreachable the dashboard says so; it never draws a browser-estimated (and possibly drifting) number.

4.3  Why you can trust it

  • Outlier trim on the price spread — the histogram shows the real distribution, not one giant bar caused by a bad row.
  • Depth gate (≥ 30 sales) on the leaderboards & scatter — only areas with enough real sales are ranked, so a fluke n=2 area or a garbage 120,000 JD/m² row can't top the board. Of ~320 areas, 89 clear the bar.
  • Median, not mean, everywhere prices are summarised — robust to luxury outliers.
  • Empty when thin — a graph with too little data renders "not enough data" rather than a misleading shape.
  • Server-computed — the numbers are identical to what the engine holds; nothing is recalculated in the browser.
